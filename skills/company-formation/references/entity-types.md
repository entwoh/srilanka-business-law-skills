# Choosing an entity type

## Comparison

| | Sole proprietorship | Partnership | Private limited company | Public company | Overseas branch |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Separate legal person | No | No | **Yes** | Yes | No — the foreign company itself |
| Limited liability | No | No | **Yes** | Yes | No |
| Registers with | Provincial Registrar of Business Names | Provincial Registrar | **ROC (eROC)** | ROC | ROC as overseas company |
| Owners | 1 | 2–20 typically | 1–50 | Unlimited | n/a |
| Company Secretary | No | No | **Mandatory** | Mandatory | Authorised representative required |
| Annual return | No | No | **Form 15** | Form 15 | Form 35 / 36 alterations |
| Audited accounts | No | No | Generally yes | Yes | Per home jurisdiction + local filings |
| Foreign ownership | Not practical | Not practical | **Yes, subject to sectoral limits** | Yes | By definition |
| Setup cost | Lowest | Low | Moderate | High | Moderate |
| Credibility with banks/clients | Low | Low | **High** | High | Varies |

## Private limited company — the default

For almost any venture intending to grow, hire, take investment or limit personal risk, a
private limited company is the answer.

- One shareholder and one director is enough (but a *separate* Company Secretary is required)
- Shareholders capped at 50
- No authorised capital ceiling — Sri Lanka uses **stated capital**, and there is no
  statutory minimum, though banks and some licences impose their own expectations
- Shares are not freely transferable to the public
- Governed by its Articles; the model Articles in the First Schedule to the Companies Act are
  available if you don't want bespoke drafting

**Choose bespoke Articles if** there is more than one shareholder, any investor, any
intention to raise capital, or any desire to control transfers, pre-emption or board
composition. The model Articles are adequate for a single-owner company and inadequate for
almost anything else.

## Sole proprietorship

Fast and cheap. Register the business name with the **Provincial** Registrar of Business
Names — this is not a ROC matter and requirements vary by province.

The trade-off is total: no separate legal personality, unlimited personal liability, business
income taxed as your personal income, and difficulty opening commercial accounts or
contracting with larger counterparties.

Sensible for a low-risk single-person service business testing an idea. Not sensible for
anything holding inventory, employing staff, or signing meaningful contracts.

## Partnership

Governed by the Partnership Ordinance plus whatever the partners agree. Registers provincially.
Partners are **jointly and severally liable** — each partner is exposed to the whole of the
firm's obligations, including those incurred by other partners.

Write a partnership agreement. Without one, the default statutory rules apply and they will
not match what the partners assumed.

Professional firms sometimes must use this form where their regulator prohibits incorporation.
Otherwise, a company is usually better.

## Public company

Required if shareholders exceed 50 or if shares or debentures are offered to the public.
Brings prospectus requirements, heavier governance, and — if listed — SEC and CSE continuing
obligations. Do not choose this for structural reasons alone; it's a substantial compliance
uplift.

## Overseas company (branch) vs subsidiary

A foreign company establishing a place of business in Sri Lanka registers as an **overseas
company** using Forms 44, 45 and 46 (registered office details, directors, and persons
authorised to accept service).

| | Branch | Subsidiary |
|---|---|---|
| Legal separation | None — parent is directly liable | Separate Sri Lankan company |
| Perception | Sometimes viewed as less committed | Usually preferred by counterparties |
| Accounts | Parent accounts may need filing | Sri Lankan accounts only |
| Exit | Form 38 notice of ceasing business | Sale of shares or winding up |

Most foreign investors incorporate a subsidiary. Branches suit representative offices,
project-specific presences, and regulated sectors where the licence is held by the parent.

## BOI enterprise

A BOI agreement is layered **on top of** an entity — you still incorporate a company, then
negotiate BOI status. It can bring tax concessions, customs treatment, and in some zones
distinct labour arrangements, in exchange for investment and employment commitments.

Worth pursuing for export-oriented manufacturing, large-scale investment, or anything in a
promoted sector. Not worth pursuing for a small domestic services business.

See [`../../foreign-investment/references/boi.md`](../../foreign-investment/references/boi.md).

## Decision shortcut

- Testing a small idea alone, low risk → sole proprietorship, convert later
- Anything with co-founders, employees, investors or real liability → **private limited company**
- Foreign parent wanting presence without separation → branch
- Foreign parent wanting a real local business → subsidiary
- Export manufacturing or large investment → subsidiary **plus** BOI
- Raising from the public → public company
