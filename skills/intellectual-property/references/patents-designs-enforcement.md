# Patents, designs, trade secrets and enforcement

## Patents

Protect inventions. Verify the criteria under Part III of the IP Act, which will require
novelty, an inventive step, and industrial applicability, with exclusions.

**Key practical points:**

- **Absolute novelty.** Public disclosure before filing generally destroys novelty. Do not
  demo, publish, pitch publicly or launch before filing. This is the mistake that ends patent
  prospects, and it is usually made unknowingly.
- **NDAs before disclosure** to investors, partners or manufacturers.
- **Territorial.** A Sri Lankan patent protects in Sri Lanka. For other markets, file there.
  Verify Sri Lanka's PCT position for a route to multiple jurisdictions.
- **Expensive and slow** relative to the size of most Sri Lankan businesses. For many
  software and services companies, trade secrets plus speed to market is the better strategy.

Verify the term, annuity requirements, and what happens on non-payment.

## Industrial designs

Protect the **appearance** of a product — shape, configuration, pattern, ornamentation — not
how it works.

Relevant to consumer products, packaging, furniture, textiles and jewellery. Registration is
at NIPO. Verify novelty requirements, term and renewal.

Note the overlap: a product's appearance may attract design protection, its brand a trade
mark, and its manual copyright. These are cumulative, not alternatives.

## Trade secrets and confidential information

Often the most practical protection for a Sri Lankan business, especially in software and
services.

Protection depends on **keeping it secret** and on contract, not registration:

- Confidentiality clauses in employment and contractor agreements
- NDAs before any disclosure to third parties
- Access controls — not everyone needs the customer list
- Marking confidential material as such
- Exit procedures when people leave

The advantage over a patent: no disclosure, no expiry, no filing cost. The disadvantage: no
protection against independent discovery or reverse engineering, and once it leaks it is gone.

## Enforcement

**Forum:** the **Commercial High Court** hears IP infringement matters.

**Remedies** typically include:
- Interim and final **injunctions**
- **Damages** or an account of profits
- **Delivery up** or destruction of infringing goods
- Costs

**Criminal provisions** exist for certain IP offences, particularly counterfeiting and piracy.
Verify scope.

**Customs** recordal can help intercept counterfeit imports. Verify the current procedure.

## Practical enforcement sequence

1. **Evidence first.** Screenshots, purchases of the infringing product, dated records. Do this
   before alerting the infringer.
2. **Verify your own position.** Is your registration in force? In the right classes? Do you
   actually own the IP, with the assignment chain intact?
3. **Letter of demand** from an Attorney-at-Law.
4. **Negotiation** — many matters resolve with a coexistence or licence arrangement.
5. **Proceedings**, with interim relief if the harm is ongoing.

Be realistic: litigation is slow and costly. For low-value infringement, a demand letter and a
commercial resolution usually beat a judgment you wait years for.
