# Share transfers and transmission

## Transfer is not issue

A transfer moves **existing** shares from one holder to another. The company receives nothing;
the selling shareholder does. No new shares are created and no one is diluted.

Consequences that follow from this:

- **No Form 6.** Form 6 is for issues. A transfer is reflected in the register of shareholders
  and surfaces in the annual return.
- **Stamp duty generally applies** to the transfer instrument.
- **Capital gains** may arise for the seller.
- The board's role is approval of the transferee, not allotment.

## Restrictions to check first

1. **The Articles** — private companies commonly restrict transfers: board approval of the
   transferee, pre-emption in favour of existing shareholders, outright prohibitions on
   transfer to competitors.
2. **Any shareholders' agreement** — tag-along, drag-along, rights of first refusal, lock-ins,
   and consent requirements.
3. **Sectoral and foreign ownership limits** if the transferee is foreign.
4. **Regulatory approvals** for licensed businesses — a change of control may need the
   regulator's consent.

Pre-emption on transfer is the trap. Many private company Articles require the seller to offer
shares to existing holders first, at a price set by a stated mechanism. Selling to an outsider
without running that process gives the other shareholders a real complaint.

## Mechanics

1. Check Articles, shareholders' agreement, and any regulatory consent requirement
2. Run pre-emption or obtain written waivers
3. Execute the share transfer instrument
4. **Pay stamp duty** on the instrument
5. Board approval of the transfer and the transferee, minuted
6. Update the **register of shareholders** — this is what actually effects the change as
   between the company and the shareholders
7. Cancel the old share certificate; issue a new one
8. Update the **beneficial ownership** register and ROC filing if the transferee crosses 10%,
   or if the transferor drops below
9. Reflect in the next annual return

## Stamp duty

Payable on the instrument of transfer. **Rates and the computation basis must be verified**
against the Stamp Duty (Special Provisions) Act No. 12 of 2006 and current regulations before
any transaction — this is a commonly cited figure that goes stale.

Unstamped or under-stamped instruments create problems with admissibility and surface at
diligence. Pay it at the time, not later.

## Transmission on death or insolvency

Transmission is a transfer by operation of law rather than by agreement.

**On death:** the shares pass to the legal personal representative on production of the grant
(probate or letters of administration). The Articles govern whether the representative can be
registered as a holder or must transfer to a beneficiary.

Sri Lankan succession can be affected by **personal laws** (Kandyan, Thesawalamai, Muslim
law), which may determine who is entitled. This is out of scope here and needs specialist
advice.

**On insolvency:** the shares vest in the trustee or liquidator.

Practical point: transmission is slow, because the grant takes time. Companies with a small
number of shareholders should consider what happens on a founder's death *before* it happens
— cross-option arrangements, insurance, and clear Articles.
